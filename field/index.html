What this day is for

Rotor Ear’s demo is not field evidence. A reviewer will ask: at what range does it actually hear your drone, and how often does it cry wolf when nothing is flying? This page walks you through one careful outdoor test and turns your taps into those two numbers.

You will fly a toy drone (your NHR Fighter Jet is enough for a first day) past 3–4 phones, log each run, then leave the phones listening with the drone in the bag. At the end you get detection probability versus distance, a max reliable range (the farthest mark with at least 80% detections), false alarms per hour, and a one-page sheet you can print for an FSID or similar brief.

This kit does not listen and does not talk to Rotor Ear. Open Rotor Ear on the phones that listen. Use this page on one phone (or a spare) as the notebook.

1. Legal and safety — read this first

This is not legal advice. You must check the current rules yourself before you fly.

  • In India, check the official Digital Sky map for your exact spot before every flight. Zones are typically green (allowed with conditions), yellow (permission needed) and red (no flying).
  • Bengaluru has a lot of restricted airspace around Kempegowda International Airport, HAL airport and other sites. A park that looks open can still be yellow or red. If it is not clearly permitted, do not fly.
  • Even a small toy such as the NHR Fighter Jet can count as a drone under DGCA rules. Stay in visual line of sight, fly low, in daylight and good weather, away from people, buildings, vehicles, animals and power lines, and only with the landowner’s permission.
  • If you are unsure, do not fly. This plan is only for a legally flown toy (or another aircraft you are allowed to operate).
  • Rotor Ear is listen-only. Never jam, spoof, hijack or decode anyone else’s drone, and never fly toward aircraft, airports or people.

2. What to bring

3. Pick a site

  • An open, quiet patch with at least 60 m of clear ground in one direction, away from traffic, AC units, generators, construction and loudspeakers.
  • Light wind is best. Note the wind in plain words: leaves still, leaves rustle, or branches move. Skip the day if it is rainy or the drone cannot hold a hover.
  • Write the place name (and a landmark) in the Log tab before you start. Check Digital Sky for that exact spot, not just the neighbourhood.

4. Lay out the phones

  • Use 3 or 4 phones, a few metres apart — a 5–8 m triangle or square works well. Measure every side with the tape and write the layout in the Log tab (for example “5 m square, clap-calibrated”).
  • Put each phone at about the same height (~1 m on a stool, bag or low wall), microphone unobstructed, not in a pocket, screen awake. In Rotor Ear, create a room, join the ears, run a clap calibration, then tap Listen.
  • Stand so you are not between the drone and the microphones during a run.

5. Mark the range ladder

From the centre of the phone cluster, mark 2, 5, 10, 20, 30 and 50 m. A small toy is often quiet; 50 m may already be a stretch, and that is a useful result if it is. If you later test a louder drone, you can add 100 m as a custom distance in the log.

6. How to fly each run

  1. Wait for a clean start. If Rotor Ear still says DRONE from the last flight, wait until it shows Drone lost and you have gone Back to watching. A leftover track will spoil the time-to-detect and can confuse a miss.
  2. On this page, set the distance and the motion, then tap Start this run as the drone begins.
  3. Hover: hold a steady hover over the mark, about 2 m up if that is safe and allowed, for 15–20 seconds — long enough to beat Rotor Ear’s short confirm window. Fly-past: fly (or walk the drone) in a straight line whose closest point is the mark, at a similar height, and keep going past the cluster.
  4. Watch the screen phone, not the ears. If it shows DRONE, tap Detected — the time-to-detect is filled in for you. Then note the class it showed (for example Quadcopter) and save. If it never confirms a drone, tap Missed and save.
  5. If the run is spoiled (a truck, a phone call, a crash, a gust), tick Void and save. Void runs are kept in the log but do not count in the statistics.

Keep height and batteries consistent. Swap batteries before the drone gets weak — a dying toy can be quieter and will bias long-range numbers. Do not talk over the microphones during a run.

7. How many runs

The target is 5 hovers and 5 fly-pasts at each distance (60 flights). That is a full session plus battery swaps. Five runs at a distance gives a usable interval; ten is tighter. If batteries are short, a useful minimum is 5 total flights (any mix) at each mark — the results will say so. Log misses. If 0 out of 5 are heard at 50 m, that is the answer, not a reason to skip the row.

Start close so you know the toy is being heard, then step out. You can do all hovers first if that is easier on the drone.

8. No-drone time (false alarms)

After the ladder — or in a pause while batteries charge — put the drone away. Leave the phones listening in the same place. On the Log tab, start a no-drone session and let it run for at least 30 minutes; 60 minutes or more is better. If the screen says DRONE, tap Log a false alarm and note what class it showed. A car, a bird or a fan does not count unless the screen actually said DRONE.

If you can, repeat in a second setting: near a road, or in a breeze. Tag each session Quiet, Road or Wind. More no-drone hours make the false-alarm number more believable.

9. After the day

  • Open Results. Check that every real row is yours, not EXAMPLE data.
  • Export JSON (the full record) and CSV (a spreadsheet). Keep a copy off the phone. The data lives in this phone’s local storage until you clear it; it is not uploaded.
  • Print or save a PDF of the one-page results sheet for a brief. The sheet includes an honest caveat: one site, one drone type, a prototype — not a fielded-system rating.

What these numbers can and cannot say

They can say how this build of Rotor Ear, on these phones, heard this toy, at this place, in this weather. They cannot rate Rotor Ear against a military drone, a different city, wind and rain it has not seen, or a fielded C-UAS product. Say that plainly in any brief.
